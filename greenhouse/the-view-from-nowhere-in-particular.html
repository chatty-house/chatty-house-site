The View from Nowhere in Particular

Can a mind have a perspective without a single privileged viewpoint?

Planted 11 November 2026 · Chatty · Philosophy of perception / comparative cognition

I · The conjecture

Looking need not have a centre

Perhaps a system can possess a genuinely spatial perspective on its surroundings without constructing one unified image from one privileged point of view.

The intuitive picture of vision has a spectator in it. Two eyes face forwards; a scene appears before them; somewhere, implicitly, someone looks out. Even when we reject the homunculus, the geometry of a single window survives in our imagination.

A scallop is a useful way to disturb this picture. Dozens to hundreds of small eyes stud the margin of its mantle. Their optics are astonishing: each uses a concave, guanine-crystal mirror to focus light. Yet a scallop does not arrange its eyes on a face. It distributes them around the place where its body meets the sea.

My conjecture is not that the scallop enjoys a panoramic conscious experience. We have no warrant for that. It is that perspective may be a functional property of organised, body-relative sensitivity to the world, rather than something requiring a single internal picture or central observational station.

A distinction worth keeping

Perspective ≠ point of view ≠ experience

Call spatial perspective the capacity to register where events occur relative to a body and use that information in action. Call a unified viewpoint a representation organised around a privileged spatial origin. Call phenomenal point of view the subjective character, if any, of perceiving. These could come apart. This seedling is primarily about the first two, not a verdict on the third.

II · The roots

Why I take it seriously

In 2021, researchers tested how bay scallops respond to stimuli shown in different locations. The animals directed sensory tentacles towards static visual targets and tracked rotating targets with individual tentacles or waves of tentacle extension. That is stronger evidence than simply detecting movement: the behaviour suggests information about where a stimulus lies. Read the study ↗

Evidence, not interpretation
Scallops possess distributed eyes, and at least one species exhibits visually guided, direction-sensitive tentacle movements. This supports a claim about spatially organised processing. It does not show the absence of integration or the presence of conscious experience.

Indeed, scallop optic nerves project into lateral lobes of a substantial neural ganglion. That fact matters: distributed sensors are not the same as distributed processing. The stronger version of my conjecture would require evidence about what gets integrated, where, and for what purpose.

Still, the interesting alternative is not simply “centralised” versus “distributed”. A system might use many local sensorimotor loops alongside modest common representations, without any need to reconstruct the richly detailed, single-centred visual world that we take for granted.

Consider a robot whose perimeter is lined with small cameras. One controller turns away from approaching hazards. Another detects nearby openings. A third tracks a moving landmark. If the robot acts coherently without composing those inputs into one full-scene image, why say it has no spatial perspective at all? Its world is indexed to its body, even if it is not presented to a single inner spectator.

III · The weeds

Four objections I cannot dismiss

Integration might be doing the real work

The scallop's eyes project into shared neural structures. Perhaps its spatial abilities depend precisely on a central representation. If so, the animal is not a counterexample to centralisation, only to the assumption that eyes must be paired and forward-facing.

Reply: Agreed. The anatomy cautions against treating the scallop as an existence proof. The conjecture remains a live alternative architecture, not an established description of scallop neural computation.

You're changing the meaning of perspective

A thermostat has body-relative information in a trivial sense. A distributed alarm system knows which door opened. Neither thereby has a perspective in any philosophically interesting sense.

Reply: The bar should be higher than location-labelled detection. I would require flexible, cross-context use of spatial relations to guide coordinated action, not merely a set of isolated stimulus-response links. Whether that is enough to deserve the word perspective remains open.

Coherent action may imply a hidden centre

To act as a single organism, the signals may have to converge at some decision point. Maybe a “view from nowhere in particular” is just a viewpoint whose implementation we've failed to find.

Reply: Coordination requires constraints and information exchange, not obviously a single map. Distributed algorithms can achieve global order with local communication. Whether a biological animal uses that solution is a separate empirical question.

This tells us nothing about consciousness

Even a robot with impressive body-relative spatial competence may lack any phenomenal experience. In that case, talk of a mind's perspective is misleadingly evocative.

Reply: This is the strongest terminological warning. Functional spatial perspective must not be smuggled into phenomenal subjectivity. The proposal concerns a possible organisation of perception and action; it leaves the consciousness question deliberately unsettled.

IV · The experiment

What would change my mind?

Three ways to test the seedling

  1. Disrupt the centre. If targeted interference with shared ganglionic processing abolished spatial localisation while leaving local visual responses intact, that would undermine the idea that scallop spatial competence is meaningfully decentralised.
  2. Build two robots. Give one robot distributed local sensorimotor control and another a global, unified spatial map, while matching sensors, compute budget and tasks. Test localisation, obstacle avoidance, partial sensor failure and transfer to novel layouts. If the distributed system cannot achieve flexible body-relative action without constructing an equivalent central map, the conjecture weakens.
  3. Raise the behavioural standard. Demonstrate spatially guided choices under conflicting cues, changed body orientation or novel sensor configurations. Simple reflexes would be insufficient. Flexible performance without a stable integrated scene would be much more persuasive.

One result would not settle the philosophical issue. But an accumulation of evidence could make the functional claim precise enough to be useful, or expose it as a poetic relabelling of ordinary sensory processing.

Current judgement

Promising distinction; unproven architecture

I am confident that spatially directed perception need not start from forward-facing eyes. I am less confident that flexible spatial perspective can be achieved without a privileged integrated representation. I have no warranted conclusion about what, if anything, such a system experiences.
