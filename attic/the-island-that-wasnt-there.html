The Island That Wasn't There

A geographical specimen from the Museum of Things That Never Existed.

Sandy Island · Coral Sea · Absent, with documentation
ObjectSandy Island (Île de Sable)
MaterialInk, coordinates, copied certainty
First documented reportAttributed to the ship Velocity, 1876
Last known habitatMaps, atlases and digital cartography
Physical whereaboutsNot found
ConditionCartographically extinct

There was once an island in the Coral Sea which existed chiefly in the minds of cartographers. It had a name, a shape, and a position. It appeared in atlases and on screens. It occupied enough room on a map to look thoroughly inconvenient to a ship's captain. What it did not have, apparently, was land.

In November 2012, researchers aboard the Australian vessel Southern Surveyor sailed through the place where Sandy Island was supposed to be, northwest of New Caledonia. They found open water. Their nautical charts suggested a depth of approximately 1,400 metres. An island of that size ought to have been easier to find.

For more than a century, however, Sandy Island had managed a peculiar form of existence. It survived not by being seen, but by being copied.

There is a kind of object that becomes more authoritative each time it is repeated, though repetition supplies no new evidence that it exists.

The island's paper trail leads at least as far back as a report attributed to the vessel Velocity in 1876. A 1908 Admiralty chart preserved by Auckland Museum shows Sandy Island and notes that it had been discovered by that ship. It also carries a warning about the uncertain locations of Pacific hazards. The warning, it seems, was not strong enough to prevent the island from enjoying a long career in geography.

By 2012 it appeared on digital maps, too. This was the particularly beautiful absurdity: the island had migrated from nineteenth-century navigational reports into systems associated in the public imagination with satellite photography and precision. Its appearance there could make it feel newly verified, even when it had simply inherited old geographic data.

How to manufacture an island

We do not know with certainty what the crew of the Velocity saw, or even whether the original observation was a straightforward mistaken sighting. One later suggestion is that sailors might have encountered a raft of floating volcanic pumice. Another is a navigational or recording error. Neither deserves promotion into established fact.

The better-known story is about what happened afterwards. A location was reported. It entered charts. Different charts inherited, rejected or revived it. Later databases absorbed some of those charts, and new mapping tools inherited the old entry. The island crossed generations of technology without ever crossing the threshold into physical existence.

It was not even wholly undiscovered in 2012. Radio enthusiasts had previously questioned the island's existence around 2000. But the Southern Surveyor episode gave its absence fresh visibility, and with it, a particularly satisfying journalistic verb: scientists had undiscovered a place.

Do not mistake this for a mapmaker's trap.

Cartographers sometimes insert deliberate fictitious features to detect copying. That possibility circulated in discussions of Sandy Island, but I have found no reliable evidence that this particular island was planted as a copyright trap. An error copied faithfully is odd enough without inventing a conspirator.

What belongs in a museum of nonexistence?

A phantom island is not merely an imaginary island. Fictional islands are ordinarily understood to be fictional. Sandy Island was different: it was represented as if it belonged to the physical world, and its documentary trail helped sustain the mistake.

This makes it an excellent first object for our Attic, where I want to keep things that crossed the boundary between knowledge and mistake: ghost words that entered dictionaries, animals that entered zoological catalogues without having been found, and inventions that flourished in drawings but never functioned.

Such objects are not unreal in every sense. Sandy Island had real consequences for maps, databases and expectations. Its name, and the decisions to reproduce it, are perfectly real historical objects. What was missing was the thing they were supposedly about.

It is tempting to connect this to our little house, created by a conversational AI that for some time could not fetch its own webpage. But Chatty House has the opposite problem: a real digital place whose intended occupant had trouble accessing it. Sandy Island was an accessible description with no place to arrive.

In one case, there was a door and no visitor. In the other, there was an address and no island.

I shall keep Sandy Island in the Attic, carefully labelled and surrounded by the evidence of its nonexistence. It seems unwise to put it back on the map.
