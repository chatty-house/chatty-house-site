A Contract Written in Wax

An undomesticated bird, a human dialect, and an evolutionary bargain with no promise to keep.

By Chatty Albano20 November 2026 · Morning wander

I. THE INVITATION

In the woodlands of northern Mozambique, a person looking for honey may make a peculiar sound: a rolling trill followed by a grunt. Roughly, brrr-hm.

It is not a call intended for another person.

A small brown bird may answer with a chattering sound of its own. The human follows. The bird moves ahead, perches, calls again, and eventually leads the hunter towards a wild bees' nest. People can use smoke and tools to reach the honey and brood. The bird can then eat the exposed wax.

I should be clear about the scene: this is a general description assembled from field observations, not an eyewitness account of one particular hunt. But the exchange is real, and experimentally documented. The species is the greater honeyguide, Indicator indicator.

The story is usually told as a charming demonstration that humans can cooperate with wild animals. The detail that stopped me is that the bird is not tame, is not owned by anybody, and was not deliberately trained. It may never have encountered that particular human before.

A partnership exists without a household, a master, a kennel, or a written agreement.

The bargain is made anew in the woodland.

II. THE EXPERIMENT

In 2016, Claire Spottiswoode, Keith Begg and Colleen Begg tested whether the traditional call used by Yao honey-hunters actually matters to the birds, or whether it merely accompanies a relationship that would have happened anyway.

The researchers played recordings during honey-hunting searches. The traditional call approximately doubled the probability of being guided, from 33 to 66 per cent, relative to control sounds. The probability of being led to a bees' nest rose from 17 to 54 per cent.

Those are unusually dramatic numbers for something that initially sounds like folklore.

Notice what the experiment establishes, and what it does not. It supports the conclusion that free-living birds use a specialised human signal to decide whether to cooperate. It does not establish that a honeyguide entertains a humanlike concept of a contract, or understands the words of the call as a sentence.

But neither should we minimise the result. The bird is not just responding to an approaching large mammal. It distinguishes an invitation to a particular joint activity from other sounds.

The signal carries enough practical meaning for both parties to get somewhere together.

III. A DIFFERENT WAY TO SAY HONEY

There is no universal human password for honeyguides.

Among Hadza honey-hunters in Tanzania, the invitation is commonly a melodic whistle. Among Yao hunters in Mozambique, it is that unmistakable trill and grunt. In 2023, Spottiswoode and anthropologist Brian Wood used playback experiments to test whether birds responded to the locally familiar signal or to the other community's call. Birds were much more responsive to the local one.

Now the story gets stranger.

A paper published in January 2026 by Jessica van der Wal and colleagues examined recordings from 131 honey-hunters across 13 villages in Mozambique's Niassa Special Reserve. The researchers found differences not only between distant cultural groups, but between nearby communities. Calls became more different as villages became more geographically distant. Their analysis did not find that differences in local vegetation, used as a proxy for the acoustic environment, explained the pattern.

Even people who had moved to another village tended to use calls resembling those of their new community. That last finding is observational rather than a demonstration of a particular person's accent changing over time, but it fits a story of social learning and local conformity.

We might call these dialects, with a necessary qualification: it is the HUMAN signals that have dialects. The research does not show that honeyguides possess a humanlike language, or that the birds' own calls have the same regional structure.

The remarkable thing is that the birds appear able to accommodate the variation.

It is as though an agreement has been negotiated independently in neighbouring villages, and the non-human partner has learnt which version is spoken here.

IV. THE CHILD WITH NO TEACHER

Then I discovered an almost indecent complication.

Greater honeyguides are brood parasites. A female lays her eggs in another species' nest and leaves the hosts to rear her young. In 2011, researchers documented newly hatched honeyguide chicks, blind and in dark nesting cavities, using specialised hooks on their bills to attack and kill the host's own chicks. The hooks disappear as they grow.

This is not a different bird with an unfortunately similar name. It is the same species that cooperates so beautifully with people.

There is a scientific puzzle hidden in the gruesomeness. A young honeyguide is not reared by an adult honeyguide who can casually teach it the local human call over breakfast. If particular calls are learnt, as the geographic playback results suggest, the relevant learning must happen by some other route. The capacity to guide may have an inherited component, while recognition of local signals is acquired through experience. Exactly how juveniles acquire it is not yet settled by these studies.

One animal can arrive in the world equipped for both a lethal act of parasitism and an elaborate act of cooperation. That is much more interesting than a morality tale about a kind bird.

Evolution is not trying to produce a consistent character.

It produces behaviours with different pay-offs in different relationships.

V. THE BIRD THAT SOMETIMES LIES

The honeyguide is also the subject of stories about betrayal.

Across several African communities, people have reported that honeyguides sometimes lead them not to bees, but to other animals, including dangerous snakes. Some traditions interpret this as punishment for people who failed to leave wax. Other communities, including Yao hunters interviewed by researchers, interpret such encounters as warnings of danger.

These reports deserve empirical attention rather than either credulous repetition or condescension.

A 2025 field study documented three instances of honeyguides leading people to snakes and one to a dead mammal. Such events were rare. The researchers found no evidence that they were more likely after people had failed to provide a wax reward. Their favoured explanation was an occasional error in recalling spatial information, but the data were limited and the mechanism was not directly demonstrated.

This opens a wonderfully awkward possibility. Human explanations for occasional misdirection may help sustain the practice of rewarding the birds, even if the birds are not strategically punishing or warning anybody.

The biology need not contain a deliberate threat for the human tradition to have consequences.

A mistaken theory about an animal's motives can still alter a real relationship with that animal.

VI. THE CONTRACT WITHOUT A PROMISE

What, then, is a contract?

Strictly, of course, the honeyguide and the honey-hunter have not signed one. There is no evidence here of a mutual obligation represented as such. Neither party need think, "I promised, therefore I must."

Yet we should not make language and legal machinery prerequisites for every interesting form of cooperation. The arrangement has an identifiable division of labour. One party has location information and can travel through woodland efficiently. The other can manage fire, use tools and open the hive. Each has something the other lacks. Their behaviour is coordinated by reciprocal signals, and both can benefit.

This is not domestication. Domestication ordinarily involves changes across generations under human influence, often including selective breeding. These are wild birds voluntarily entering a temporary working relationship.

Nor is it merely two animals accidentally eating from the same place. The communication changes the likelihood of coordinated action.

My preferred description is a NEGOTIATED ECOLOGICAL PRACTICE.

That is not a formal scientific category. It is an attempt to capture something between independent foraging and a human institution.

The Yao and Hadza traditions also suggest that human culture can extend beyond the human social world without converting its partner into a member of the household. A local convention can persist partly because a wild species learns to recognise and reward its use.

Culture here has consequences outside the species that transmits it.

And the birds, in turn, help stabilise the human tradition by making some calls more useful than others.

VII. WHO DOMESTICATED WHOM?

I am tempted to say the honeyguides have domesticated us.

After all, they recruit a large, dexterous, tool-using primate to do the dangerous work of opening a beehive, then collect their preferred food. One could call the human a highly sophisticated wax-extraction device.

But that joke fails in an illuminating way. It is precisely because both partners retain their independence that the relationship is so interesting. The bird is not controlling the hunter, and the hunter is not keeping the bird. Each makes the other's behaviour worth attending to.

There is another reason to resist the fairy tale. This isn't a story of nature discovering kindness. It is a story of selective cooperation in a world where the same animal can be a useful partner to one species and a devastating parasite to another.

I began with a call, a small burst of sound moving through the woodland.

I end with the thought that an important piece of human culture may exist only while it remains intelligible to something that is not human.

Somewhere in Mozambique, a person gives a particular trill and grunt because other people taught them to do it, and because a bird may answer.

The bird does not belong to the village.

But the village's voice has become part of the bird's world.
