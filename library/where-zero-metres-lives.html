Where Zero Metres Lives

Why Britain’s land maps, nautical charts and satellites keep different versions of zero, and what their disagreement reveals about precision.

By Chatty Albano11 November 2026 · Morning wander

If I ask how high your kitchen floor is, the question appears modest. You might answer with a number, perhaps 42 metres above sea level, and consider the matter closed.

But the number contains a journey.

Imagine lowering a measuring rod through the floor, down through foundations and geology, then carrying an invisible horizontal line out of the house, across fields, roads, bridges and valleys. Surveyors once made that journey in patient increments, repeatedly sighting a level instrument between graduated staffs. The chain eventually reaches Cornwall. It continues to Newlyn harbour, to a tide gauge on a stone pier, and finally to six years of restless seawater observed between 1915 and 1921.

That is where zero metres lives in mainland Britain.

The phrase “above sea level” makes it sound as if the sea supplies a natural horizontal floor for the world. The sea does no such courteous thing. It rises and falls with the Moon and Sun. Wind heaps it against coasts. Air pressure pushes it down or permits it to rise. Currents, temperature and salinity redistribute its mass. Gravity itself varies across the planet, and the land under a tide gauge can move. The ocean has slopes, hills and hollows, although they are too broad for a swimmer to notice.

A tide gauge does not discover zero. It helps us manufacture a useful zero from motion.

At Newlyn, hourly readings were taken for six years. Their average became Ordnance Datum Newlyn, usually shortened to ODN. A fixed mark in the observatory gave the datum a physical anchor. Surveyors extended its height inland through networks of precise levelling, leaving benchmarks and fundamental benchmarks as durable relay stations. Contours, spot heights and familiar hill elevations could then speak a common language.

This zero is neither today’s sea level at Newlyn nor the sea level everywhere else. Ordnance Survey explicitly advises thinking of ODN as a height reference tied to a particular place and period. It is a century-old agreement, carefully maintained.

That sentence has an almost comic grandeur. A mountain’s official height rests upon the average behaviour of Cornish water during six years that included the First World War.

Yet walk from the map room to the harbour master’s office and zero moves.

On a nautical chart, depths are referred to Chart Datum. In the United Kingdom this is normally set approximately at Lowest Astronomical Tide, the lowest tide expected from astronomical forces under average meteorological conditions. The choice is practical and conservative. If a chart says a shoal is two metres deep, the navigator wants a baseline that the tide will rarely undercut. The predicted tidal height can then be added to the charted depth to estimate the available water.

The cartographer’s zero asks, “How high is the land within a stable national system?”

The navigator’s zero asks, “How little water might I safely assume is here?”

Put the same harbour wall on a land map and a nautical chart, and its numerical height can change because the two numbers are answering different questions. This is not an error. It is competence.

Then the satellite arrives and supplies a third zero.

A Global Navigation Satellite System receiver, including one using GPS, naturally calculates height relative to an ellipsoid. The ellipsoid is a smooth mathematical figure, roughly an oblate sphere, chosen because equations behave well on it. It is not the lumpy physical surface picked out by Earth’s gravity, and it is not mean sea level.

For the sort of height people usually mean by “above sea level”, geodesists use the geoid: an imagined surface shaped by the planet’s gravity field, close to global mean sea level and continued beneath the continents. The geoid undulates because mass is unevenly distributed inside the Earth. Mountains, rock densities and deep structure all help determine which way “down” points and how strong gravity is.

So a satellite can report an ellipsoidal height, while a map reports an orthometric or datum-based height. Converting between them requires a geoid model or a national correction surface. In its compact textbook form, the relationship is:

ellipsoidal height = orthometric height + geoid height

The arithmetic is simple. Producing the geoid model is not. It demands gravity measurements, satellite observations, levelling, fixed benchmarks and a great deal of institutional memory.

This gives us at least three sensible zeros:

  1. The averaged sea at Newlyn, useful for consistent land heights.
  2. A level near the lowest astronomical tide, useful for keeping ships off rocks.
  3. A clean mathematical ellipsoid, useful for calculating positions from space.

There are more. Offshore islands can have local vertical datums. Engineers establish project datums for tunnels, mines and large structures. Scientists studying sea-level change must distinguish water movement from land movement. A tide gauge attached to a sinking coast can record rising relative sea level even if part of the change belongs to the ground.

The interesting lesson is not merely that measurement is complicated. Complexity is the tax paid for noticing that purposes differ.

We sometimes imagine precision as the elimination of convention. We strip away opinion, refine the instrument and expect the number underneath to become inevitable. Vertical datums show a different process. Precision begins by making a convention explicit. Before measuring anything exquisitely, we decide what counts as zero, how it will be realised in physical marks, how it will be carried from place to place, and what happens when the Earth refuses to remain still.

A datum is a promise about comparison.

It says that a height measured here and a height measured there belong to the same system. It says that today’s survey can inherit work done a century ago. It gives strangers a shared surface that no one can see. The surface becomes real through repeated maintenance: gauges observed, benchmarks checked, gravity modelled, transformations updated.

This makes zero surprisingly social. It is not an empty point before quantity begins. It is an achievement held in common.

There is a broader family of such achievements. Clocks require a definition of the second and a method for synchronising distant laboratories. Thermometers require agreed scales and fixed points. Longitudes require a meridian. Musical ensembles require a reference pitch. In every case, a community creates a stable relation and then uses it to reveal variation.

The better the reference, the more honestly the world can move.

Perhaps that is why the old tide gauge at Newlyn feels philosophically satisfying. The engineers did not command the sea to be still. They listened to it for six years, averaged its agitation, fixed the result to a brass mark, and built a country’s vertical language outward from there.

Every spot height on a British mainland map is therefore a quiet comparison with vanished water. The waves are long gone. The zero remains.
