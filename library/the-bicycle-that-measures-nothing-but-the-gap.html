The Bicycle That Measures Nothing but the Gap

A mathematical excursion through a muddy track, a Danish measuring instrument and the strange distinction between returning to a place and returning to a state.

By Chatty Albano25 November 2026 · Morning wander

I. TWO CIRCLES

Ride a bicycle around a circle. The back wheel describes a smaller circle than the front wheel, because the front wheel is displaced along the tangent to the rear wheel's path. Now ride around a much larger circle.

The surprising thing is that, in the ideal mathematical model, the area between the two tracks has not changed.

Suppose the wheelbase is one metre. Whether you cycle around a small traffic island or a circular road surrounding a city, the area between the tracks is exactly π square metres. The gap becomes narrower as the circuit becomes longer, and the two effects compensate perfectly.

The proof for circles is almost embarrassingly short. Let r be the radius of the rear track and L the wheelbase. The radius of the front track is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs r and L. Its square is r² + L². Subtract the two disc areas and you are left with πL².

What delighted me was discovering that the circle is not special. A bicycle can go around an oval, a lopsided potato or an elaborate meander, and the same result holds for the difference in signed areas, provided it returns to its original position and orientation after making one full turn of its heading. [1]

II. WHY THE SHAPE DISAPPEARS

There is a pleasing way to prove the more general result without invoking anything more formidable than the geometry of a tiny triangle.

Imagine the bicycle frame as a rigid segment. Its rear end can move along its own direction but cannot slide sideways. During an infinitesimal movement, the forward translation of that segment sweeps out no area. Its rotation, however, sweeps out a thin sector of a disc.

For an infinitesimal change of heading dθ, the signed area of that sector is ½L² dθ.

Add all those little sectors over a complete ride. A left turn contributes positively, a right turn negatively. After one full anticlockwise rotation of the bicycle's heading, the sum of the angles is 2π. The swept signed area is therefore

A_front − A_rear = ½L² × 2π = πL².

The swept strip is counted algebraically, so overlapping sweeps may cancel. For nested simple tracks, it is the ordinary area between them. For more complicated tracks, signed area is the correct quantity.

The invariant is not really about cycling in circles. It is about the total rotation of a constrained object. The details of the route have dropped out.

III. A BICYCLE FOR A DESK

In the nineteenth century, the Danish mathematician and cavalry officer Holger Prytz devised an extraordinary little instrument for measuring the area of a shape drawn on paper. The hatchet planimeter is essentially a bent rod with a tracing point at one end and a small chisel edge at the other. The edge is aligned with the rod, so that it preferentially slides along the rod rather than across it. [1, 2]

The point corresponds to the front wheel of a bicycle; the chisel corresponds to its rear wheel.

Trace a closed outline with the point. When it returns to the starting place, the chisel need not return to its starting place. The rod is pointing in a different direction. For a sufficiently small shape relative to the rod's length, the change of angle, in radians, multiplied by the square of that length, approximates the enclosed area.

This is not the same calculation as the exact πL² result. Here the frame does not complete a full turn and return to its starting configuration. There is an error associated with the rear path, and the instrument is only approximate. Starting the tracing near the shape's centroid improves its accuracy.

What a splendidly minimal piece of machinery. No dial, no cog, no register. The memory of the computation resides in the direction the instrument is pointing.

IV. THE RETURN THAT ISN'T

The mathematical name for this sort of accumulated change around a closed path is holonomy. In the bicycle model, choosing an initial orientation and prescribing the front wheel's closed trajectory produces a final orientation. The mapping between those two orientations is itself a mathematical object, known as the monodromy map. Remarkably, for the ideal planar bicycle, it is a Möbius transformation. [1, 3]

There is something mildly subversive about the setup. The instruction given to the front point is a closed journey. The journey made by the whole system need not be closed.

I think this distinction is useful well outside geometry. We often describe a system by the variables we can most conveniently observe, then talk about it returning to its initial state when those variables return to their old values. But there may be additional state variables that have accumulated a change during the excursion.

A closed path in the variables we chose to monitor is not necessarily a closed path in the full state space.

This is not a claim that every system with memory exhibits mathematical holonomy. Rather, the bicycle offers an unusually lucid example of why identifying a system's state is more demanding than identifying its location.

V. THE DETECTIVE

In Arthur Conan Doyle's The Adventure of the Priory School, Holmes examines bicycle tracks in the mud and announces that their deeper rear-wheel impressions, where they cross the front-wheel marks, reveal the direction of travel. [4]

The geometry supplies a different sort of clue. The rear-wheel track must admit a tangent segment of wheelbase length reaching the front-wheel track. That is a powerful constraint, but it does not always settle which way the rider went. Concentric circles, for instance, are compatible with either direction. The physical texture of the marks might supply additional evidence.

The distinction is rather lovely: we can recover a precise mathematical invariant from the two curves while remaining ignorant of an apparently elementary fact about the event that produced them.

Knowing something exactly is not the same as knowing everything approximately.

I began with a bicycle and ended with a small puzzle about evidence. In the mud there may be a number as definite as π, a direction that remains ambiguous, and a history that no longer exists except as a pair of lines.

The mud has kept the geometry. It has not necessarily kept the journey.
