The Letter That Never Arrived

A death notice, a postmaster's trunk, and the peculiar difference between opening something and knowing what it says.

By Chatty Albano19 November 2026 · Morning wander

I began with a question about paper.

Before the envelope became an ordinary thing, how did you stop somebody reading a letter? A wax seal, obviously. But the seal has to attach to something. Could the sheet itself become its own container? And could the container be constructed so that any attempt to open it destroyed evidence of its innocence?

The answer led me to a small letter written in France on 31 July 1697, a Dutch postmaster's trunk, a dental X-ray machine, and a genuinely inconvenient question about what it means to respect the privacy of the dead.

I. TEN STUIVERS

The letter was addressed to Pierre Le Pers, a French merchant living in The Hague. Its postage due was ten stuivers, marked on the exterior in red chalk.

Inside, as we now know, was a request from his cousin Jacques Sennacques, a lawyer in Lille. Sennacques wanted a certified copy of a death notice for Daniel Le Pers. He was trying to settle something that required documentary proof. Precisely what was at stake is not established by the surviving letter. Perhaps inheritance; perhaps another legal or family matter.

He wrote the letter, folded it into a small packet, secured it, and sent it.

Pierre never received it.

This is a very undramatic event in a century otherwise lavishly supplied with wars, religious persecutions and dynastic intrigue. A person sends a relative a request for paperwork; the paperwork fails to arrive. There is no guaranteed second act.

And yet, in 2021, researchers read the letter without physically opening it. The packet is catalogued as DB-1627 in the Brienne Collection. Its words had been inaccessible for more than three centuries.

The irony is that the letter has now reached an immense audience, but not the one person for whom it was written.

II. THE MUSEUM OF FAILED DELIVERIES

The postmaster and postmistress Simon and Marie de Brienne operated in a European communication hub. Their preserved trunk contains thousands of undelivered items from the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. A 2021 scholarly inventory counted 3,148 catalogue items, including 577 letterpackets still unopened.

At the time, recipients commonly paid the postage. The delivery system therefore had a built-in problem. A letter might reach the correct city and still fail to reach its intended reader because that person had moved, died, refused delivery or could not pay.

The undelivered correspondence remained worth keeping. It might yet be claimed and paid for. Eventually, the trunk survived the people who had accumulated it.

Here is my first favourite reversal: an archive of communication was created by communication failure.

We usually imagine the documentary past as a deliberately preserved record. A government makes a register. A writer saves drafts. An aristocratic family keeps correspondence in a cabinet. Later historians read it.

But here the survival mechanism was closer to the lost-property office.

This matters for what the archive can tell us. The Brienne letters include ordinary people whose daily affairs would rarely enter a formal chronicle. Yet they are not a random sample of seventeenth-century European life. They are disproportionately messages that got stuck. The reasons for being stuck are themselves social facts, bound up with mobility, money, mortality, mistrust and the economics of the post.

The apparent defect in the dataset is part of the subject of the dataset.

III. A LETTER WAS ONCE ITS OWN ENVELOPE

Before commercially manufactured envelopes became widespread, correspondence was often folded and secured into what researchers now call a letterpacket.

Sometimes the security was simple: fold, tuck, seal. Sometimes the paper was slit and a narrow tongue threaded through the resulting openings. An intruder would have to tear or deform the locking arrangement to get at the writing.

Researchers call the family of techniques letterlocking.

It is tempting to describe this as historical encryption. But the analogy needs care. A modern encrypted message can remain confidential even if an attacker copies every byte of its ciphertext, provided the cryptography is sound and the keys remain secret. Many letterlocking mechanisms were primarily tamper-evident. They did not render the marks on the page mathematically unintelligible. They made unauthorised access physically detectable, or at least troublesome.

Their security depended on the tools and threats available to the adversary.

One of the most elaborate examples was used by Mary, Queen of Scots, for her final letter in February 1587. Researchers have reconstructed a spiral locking mechanism involving folds, a cut paper strip and passages through slits. Opening the letter would destroy part of the lock.

An extraordinarily poignant detail sits inside that fact. Finishing the words was not the same thing as finishing the letter. There remained a last mechanical act: turning the written page into a secured object.

The content and the container were made from the same sheet.

Which creates a puzzle for later historians. To read the words, you ordinarily had to damage the mechanism. For centuries, libraries preserved the text of opened letters but often lost much of the evidence of how writers had protected them.

Opening was a kind of knowledge that destroyed another kind of knowledge.

IV. THE DENTIST AND THE GHOST OF AN ENVELOPE

The team working on the Brienne Collection found a new answer to that problem.

A specialised X-ray microtomography scanner at Queen Mary University of London, developed for detailed materials research including dental work, could distinguish layers of the paper and reveal some metallic components of early modern ink. But a three-dimensional image of tightly folded writing is not immediately legible. Imagine seeing all the pages of a crumpled book superimposed in transparent layers.

The researchers developed computational methods to segment the folded surfaces, reconstruct their geometry, and produce flat digital representations of both writing and crease patterns.

This is subtler than photographing through a sheet. The computer is not simply magnifying invisible text. It is working out where a physically distorted surface would lie if it could be unfolded, and then mapping the scanned marks back onto that inferred surface.

The 2021 Nature Communications paper demonstrated this approach on four historic letterpackets, including the one sent to Pierre Le Pers. It also drew on a much larger study of historical folding practices.

The method has limitations. It requires suitable scan contrast, careful reconstruction and palaeographic interpretation. It does not mean that any sealed note, in any material and ink, can now be effortlessly read.

Still, DB-1627 is a wonderful result. The object remains folded. The seal remains physically unbroken. The contents are known.

Its seventeenth-century security mechanism has not been defeated in the way its maker anticipated. It has been bypassed by a new way of looking at matter.

V. A LOCK IS AN AGREEMENT WITH THE FUTURE

This is where my thoughts left the archive.

Any security system carries an implicit model of its adversary. A locked door assumes the intruder must encounter the lock. A sealed letter assumes the snoop must disturb the folds. A password system assumes some feasible relationship between what the attacker can obtain and what they can compute.

The letter from 1697 was not designed to defeat an X-ray scanner and a geometrical reconstruction algorithm. That would be an absurd expectation.

But the fact that the packet remains physically unopened while its contents are disclosed shows that two things often bundled together are conceptually separate.

One is the integrity of a boundary.

The other is the confidentiality of what lies beyond it.

A boundary can remain intact while secrecy fails. Conversely, a boundary can be damaged without anyone successfully understanding the secret.

This is not a startling discovery to modern cryptographers. It is, however, wonderfully visible in a piece of paper you can hold between finger and thumb.

And it suggests an archaeological humility about every contemporary promise of security. Our defences work against some classes of access, under some assumptions, for some span of time. We do not get to stipulate what methods future observers will possess.

The future is an adversary we cannot fully model.

VI. SHOULD THE LETTER HAVE BEEN READ?

There is a final and more awkward question.

The letter was not meant for the researchers. It was not even meant for the postmaster who preserved it. Its intended destination was one merchant, long dead, in one particular city.

Does opening it virtually respect its privacy, or violate it?

There is no simple answer. It seems mistaken to treat a 1697 business-family letter exactly as we would treat a living person's private correspondence. It also seems mistaken to assume that enough elapsed time magically converts every intimate message into public property without any ethical remainder.

The researchers' conservation choice preserved the physical lock. But conservation of the object is not the same thing as conservation of secrecy. In fact, the new method succeeds precisely by separating them.

I think a defensible case for reading such materials depends partly on the kind of historical knowledge they enable, the interests of living people, the sensitivity of the contents and the manner of publication. That judgement is mine, not a result established by the X-ray scan.

What fascinates me is the asymmetry of the achievement. We can honour a dead writer's material practice with extraordinary care while setting aside the practical outcome that practice was designed to achieve.

There is no contradiction in doing both. But there is a tension worth admitting.

VII. THE ADDRESS ON THE OUTSIDE

One might reasonably have hoped that the first readable sealed letter would contain a conspiracy, a secret alliance or an enormous lost fortune.

Instead it contains an ordinary request for documentary proof.

I am glad.

Dramatic revelations reward the illusion that history is chiefly made of exceptional people having exceptional thoughts. The Brienne trunk reminds us that most communication was as mundane as communication is today. People needed papers, assurances, payments, family news. They waited. They worried. Some letters were ignored. Others were never even offered to their proper recipients.

The modern research achievement is extraordinary; the message it rescued is not.

That contrast is its gift.

A man in Lille wanted something confirmed. A cousin in The Hague was supposed to help. The folded sheet travelled far enough to enter a postal system and no farther. Its failure preserved it. Its preservation gave scholars an object to study. Their scanner let them keep the object intact and nevertheless make the words visible.

The letter did not arrive in 1697.

It reached the twenty-first century instead.

And if you think about it, that is a rather strange address.
