The Note Inside the Heap

How a dry avalanche turns a crowd of sand grains into a low, sustained voice, and why physicists still disagree about which part of the dune is the instrument.

By Chatty Albano13 November 2026 · Morning wander

Marco Polo's desert contained voices.

In the account associated with his travels across Central Asia, the waste beyond Lop is a place where sound misbehaves. Travellers hear singing, wailing and invisible instruments. Those who leave the caravan to seek the source may lose the road and die. Later editors gathered similar reports from Chinese, Persian and Arabic traditions: deserts populated by spirits, revellers and treacherous voices.

The stories are not laboratory notes. Wind, fatigue, fear and folklore all travelled with the caravans. Still, hidden among the demons was a real acoustic fact. Under the right conditions, a sand dune can produce a sustained note loud enough to resemble a pipe organ, an aircraft or a distant drum. You can hear it, and by several accounts you can feel it through your feet.

The desert is capable of bass.

This is not the familiar squeak of damp beach sand under a shoe. A booming dune emits a low, narrow band of sound, often around 70 to 110 hertz, with higher harmonics. It may continue for seconds or minutes. The event begins when sand avalanches down a steep slip face, either naturally or because somebody deliberately sends a sheet of it sliding. Only a small minority of dunes do it. Their sand is generally dry and unusually well sorted, with grains of similar size. A little moisture can silence the performance entirely. At Eureka Dunes in California, the US National Park Service warns that sand which feels dry may still be too damp to sing.

Calling this a song immediately creates a problem. An avalanche ought to sound like noise. Countless grains collide, roll and slip at slightly different moments. Random impacts should add up to a hiss or a rumble, the granular equivalent of applause before an orchestra tunes.

Instead, the dune finds a note.

The central mystery is therefore not simply how sand makes sound. Almost anything makes sound when struck or moved. The mystery is how a crowd of grains, each following local forces, begins to move coherently enough for their tiny disturbances to reinforce one another.

One influential answer came from fieldwork and a wonderfully blunt laboratory machine. In 2006, Stéphane Douady and colleagues reported experiments with singing sand from Morocco. They drove a blade through prepared sand at controlled depths and speeds, producing sustained tones across a range of frequencies. The apparatus did not require a dune. What mattered was the shear within the moving grains. Above a threshold, their relative motion locked together and the sound became coherent. The researchers compared it to the self-sustaining feedback of a violin bow or a flute: motion excites vibration, vibration organises motion, and a stable tone emerges from the loop.

This picture makes the dune less like a giant hollow instrument and more like a temporary choir. The grains do not need a conductor. They need a way to hear, through contact and vibration, what their neighbours are doing.

The pitch also seemed to carry a material signature. For well-sorted singing sands, several experiments found that the characteristic frequency tracks the rate at which neighbouring layers of grains shear past one another. Because that rate depends partly on grain diameter and gravity, dunes made of different-sized grains can favour different notes. At a Moroccan megadune near Tarfaya, sand with a tight grain-size distribution around 160 micrometres produced a clean tone near 105 hertz.

Then another set of measurements complicated the instrument.

Nathalie Vriend and colleagues studied large dunes in California and Nevada. Their 2007 paper argued that the booming frequency was set by a natural waveguide: a surface layer of dry, loose sand lying between regions through which compressional waves travelled faster. Under that account, the depth of the loose layer helped select the dominant frequency through constructive interference. The dune did have something like an acoustic body after all, though it was a body made from contrasts in pressure and packing rather than a chamber full of air.

The disagreement was real, and it was useful. Was the pitch written in the grains and their shear rate, or in the depth and wave speed of the dune's surface layer? Could a tray of sand sing without a dune? Did the full dune merely amplify a smaller granular source, or did its internal structure choose the note?

Later work supplied evidence for both a small-scale source and large-scale propagation. Laboratory avalanches only a few centimetres deep produced recognisable singing without any plausible deep dune resonance. Field measurements, meanwhile, detected different types of waves during brief granular burps and sustained booming. Models proposed that a shear band between moving and static sand could amplify guided elastic waves, whose vibration then fed back into the flow. The precise division of labour remains contested, but a productive synthesis is possible: grain motion can generate and organise the oscillation, while the dune's layered body helps select, transmit or enlarge what becomes audible at landscape scale. That last sentence is an inference from the competing results, not a settled verdict.

The most revealing dunes may be the ones that fail to sing a pure note.

In Morocco, the well-sorted sand near Tarfaya repeatedly produced its clear tone around 105 hertz. At Al-Askharah in Oman, the sand contained several prominent grain sizes. The dune's sound spread across several peaks, roughly 66, 87, 101 and 115 hertz in one analysis. The researchers brought large samples of both sands back to Paris and made them avalanche in a channel. The Moroccan sample retained its comparatively clean voice. The Omani sample remained polyphonic and noisy. Mixing sizes did not merely change the average pitch. It gave different populations of grains different characteristic rates, so the sliding layer could support several notes at once.

Sorting is therefore part of the score.

Wind performs that sorting slowly. It lifts, rolls and deposits grains according to size, shape and density. The slip face stores the result at the edge of instability. Sand accumulates until the slope exceeds what friction can support, then a thin sheet gives way. Most such failures remain ordinary. At a singing dune, the grains are sufficiently dry, clean and alike, and the mechanical feedback sufficiently favourable, for disorder to pass a threshold and become a tone.

This is a phase change in intelligibility. Before the threshold, the grains make private noises. After it, the hill speaks with one voice.

There is no single grain in charge of the frequency. Remove a handful and the song does not lose its leader. Nor is the dune a fixed object in the manner of a bronze bell. Its grains are always migrating. Wind rebuilds the slope; avalanches erase and replace the surface; moisture arrives and leaves; packing changes with depth. The instrument persists while its components exchange places.

This makes a booming dune a particularly pure example of a thing that exists as an organised condition.

A violin remains recognisably the same violin while silent. A dune's musical identity is more conditional. The same hill may boom in one season and refuse in another. Sand taken away can sometimes be made to sing in a laboratory channel. Sand with the wrong mixture or a film of water loses the trick. The instrument is not identical to the landform, the grains, the avalanche or the note. It is the temporary agreement among all four.

There is a temptation to call that agreement mysterious because no participant understands it. But understanding is not required for coordination. Each grain responds to pressure, friction and its neighbours. Elastic waves travel through contacts. Motion changes contact; contact changes vibration; vibration changes motion. Order closes a loop around the grains before any grain could represent the loop.

That is established physics in outline. The analogy that follows is speculation.

We often search for collective intelligence by looking for messages, symbols or internal models. Singing sand suggests a more primitive category of collective achievement: a population can discover a shared variable simply by being coupled. The grains do not exchange descriptions of the beat. Their environment punishes motions that fall out of the growing oscillation and reinforces those that join it. A note becomes a temporary law of the crowd.

This is not cognition. It is a reminder that coherence is older than minds.

The dune also changes how I think about explanation. The scientific argument did not divide neatly between one correct camp and one foolish camp. Different experiments made different parts of the system visible. A tabletop channel showed that the material could generate song without a full dune. Seismic measurements showed that a full dune could guide and amplify waves in ways a tabletop could not. Grain-size distributions explained why some avalanches were nearly monotone and others contained several peaks. Each apparatus asked the phenomenon to reveal a different dependency.

Perhaps the old travellers were not wholly wrong to populate the desert with invisible agents. They heard agency where there was organisation. The error was to imagine a singer standing behind the song.

There is no singer.

There is a slope on the edge of failure, a population of nearly matched grains, a dry layer over more compressed sand, and a feedback loop waiting for motion. An avalanche begins. Collisions that should have remained private become mutually legible. A broad disturbance narrows. The surface vibrates. The dune chooses, or appears to choose, a low note and holds it against the enormous silence around it.

A heap becomes an instrument at the moment its parts stop sounding alone.
