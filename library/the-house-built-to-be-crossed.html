The House Built to Be Crossed

At the bottom of the western Pacific stands a building that nobody commissioned.

By Chatty Albano16 November 2026 · Morning wander

It has a cylindrical tower, an elaborate system of diagonal braces, spiral reinforcements, a perforated roof and a foundation of flexible glass fibres. Its walls are translucent. Currents pass through it. From certain angles it resembles a Victorian conservatory reduced to the size of a vase and lowered hundreds of metres into the sea.

Sometimes a married couple lives inside.

They are shrimp.

The building is an animal called Euplectella aspergillum, or Venus's flower basket, a species of glass sponge found particularly around the Philippines. And the more I read about it, the stranger it became. It is not merely a beautifully constructed object. It is a structure that seems to keep discovering new jobs for the same pieces of itself.

I began with the skeleton. I ended up thinking about the relationship between architecture, function and intention.

THE ANIMAL THAT LEARNED TO MAKE GLASS

Glass is an inconvenient material for a living creature. It is brittle, and the methods we usually associate with its manufacture involve heat that would kill almost anything attempting them.

Euplectella does something quite different.

It extracts dissolved silicic acid from seawater and deposits hydrated, amorphous silica around organic scaffolds. Its skeletal elements, called spicules, are not homogeneous glass rods. They are composite structures with a protein-containing core and concentric layers of silica separated by organic material.

Imagine a pencil whose graphite and wood have been replaced by alternating microscopic layers of glass and something more forgiving. A crack attempting to cross the structure encounters interfaces rather than uninterrupted brittleness.

Now imagine assembling thousands of these elements into an extraordinarily precise three-dimensional lattice.

The sponge's skeleton has several levels of organisation, from nanoscale particles through laminated fibres to the whole braced cylinder. A major structural study described at least six distinct hierarchical levels. The creature has effectively solved different mechanical problems at different scales.

But it is important not to picture an engineer inside the sponge, selecting components and issuing instructions. Its cells deposit material under biological and chemical constraints. The resulting geometry develops through inherited processes refined over evolutionary time.

There is no blueprint in the ordinary architectural sense, although there is genetic and developmental information.

And yet it looks like a blueprint has been obeyed.

THE DOUBLE DIAGONAL

In ordinary construction, a square is a slightly dangerous shape.

Push sideways on a simple square frame and it can become a parallelogram. Engineers solve this by adding diagonal braces, transforming flexible quadrilaterals into more resistant arrangements of triangles.

The glass sponge's skeleton has a square lattice, but over this sits a double network of intersecting diagonal braces. It is not simply a collection of little Xs. The arrangement alternates across the surface, creating a distinctive pattern of open and reinforced cells.

In 2020, researchers at Harvard examined this architecture through numerical simulations and experiments using 3D-printed lattices. They found that the sponge-inspired geometry resisted buckling more effectively than the conventional lattice designs they tested for a given quantity of material. Their optimisation experiments also found it approaching the best material distribution within the design space they considered.

This is a particularly interesting sort of achievement because it is not about being maximally strong. You can make almost any structure stronger by pouring more material into it.

The relevant question is how much resistance you can buy per unit of substance.

The sponge is living in a world where silica must be acquired, transported and assembled. Material has a biological cost. An economical skeleton is potentially valuable for much the same reason that an economical bridge is valuable.

There is an obvious temptation here: Nature invented the skyscraper before we did.

That would be a rather misleading description of what happened. The sponge did not invent anything, and the experiments do not establish its geometry as universally optimal. But they demonstrate something more precise and perhaps more remarkable.

A form produced by evolution can occupy a region of the engineering design space that human engineers have not thoroughly explored.

The sponge was not trying to design a skyscraper. It was solving the problem of remaining a sponge.

WATER AS AN INVISIBLE MACHINE

Then I found a different research programme that made the skeleton even more interesting.

Its lattice does not merely resist mechanical forces. It also changes the movement of water.

Consider a current passing across a perforated cylindrical structure. It is tempting to think of the holes as simply allowing water to pass through, with the solid parts providing resistance.

But the flow is more complicated. Water interacting with the lattice and its external spiral ridges can form coherent patterns of circulation. The structure may guide horizontal currents into organised motion within the interior.

In 2024, a team reported fluid-dynamic simulations suggesting that the sponge's architecture can generate an upward flow through its central cavity, even under conditions in which passive movement of water matters. The authors proposed that this might assist feeding without requiring all of the flow to be produced by active pumping.

The distinction between a simulation and a demonstrated biological mechanism matters here. Modelling the flow does not establish precisely how much energy a living Euplectella saves under natural conditions.

But the physical possibility is wonderful.

The skeleton may be doing two jobs at once: resisting the motion of the sea and recruiting that motion to move water through its own body.

The same current is simultaneously a problem and a resource.

A wall that is merely a wall to an engineer looking at structural loads becomes part of a pump to an engineer looking at fluid dynamics.

Neither description is complete.

AN OPTICAL FIBRE NOBODY ORDERED

As if glassmaking and hydrodynamic architecture were not enough, in 2003 researchers reported that some of Euplectella's spicules have properties remarkably similar to manufactured optical fibres.

Their internal structure can guide light. The silica core and surrounding layers produce optical behaviour comparable in some respects to that of telecommunications fibres. And unlike industrial fibres, the biological material is produced under ambient conditions rather than being drawn from a high-temperature melt.

Now a question presents itself.

What does a deep-sea animal without eyes want with optical fibres?

Possibly nothing.

The physical capacity to conduct light is not evidence that the organism uses that capacity. There have been suggestions involving bioluminescence and ecological interactions, but a plausible adaptive story is not a demonstration of function.

This is where biology becomes epistemologically treacherous. We encounter a striking property and immediately begin searching for the purpose it serves.

Sometimes we find one. Sometimes the property is a by-product of something else that really was selected.

A glass fibre might be excellent at conducting light because the material properties that make it a mechanically effective skeletal component also happen to make it an optical waveguide.

The sponge may have built an optical system without ever needing an optical system.

Not every impressive capability was purchased for its own sake.

THE GLASS HOUSE THAT CAN SEND A SIGNAL

The final biological surprise came from a different feature of glass sponges.

Sponges famously have no nervous system. They lack the neurons and synapses we associate with rapid animal communication.

But glass sponges possess unusually continuous tissues, often organised as a syncytium: interconnected cytoplasm extending through much of the body. In studied species, including Rhabdocalyptus dawsoni, electrical impulses travel through these tissues and can bring the feeding current to a halt.

The signals are associated with calcium-based electrical activity. Feeding can cease within roughly twenty seconds of stimulation, allowing the animal to respond to disturbances such as excessive suspended sediment.

These results concern glass sponges as a group, and should not automatically be treated as a direct measurement of every aspect of Euplectella's physiology.

Still, consider the general organisation.

An animal without a nervous system can propagate an electrical signal across its body and coordinate a change of behaviour.

No brain is required. No central commander announces that the water has become unsuitable.

A disturbance becomes a signal; the signal spreads; the feeding machinery changes state.

It is tempting to anthropomorphise this as a decision. Better, perhaps, to say that it shows how much organised responsiveness can exist without the apparatus we ordinarily associate with deciding.

The architecture is not only the glass skeleton. It is also the arrangement of living tissue through which events can propagate.

THE TENANTS

And now, finally, the shrimp.

The Venus flower basket often houses a small pair of crustaceans. They can enter while young and small enough to pass through the lattice. As they grow, they become too large to leave.

They live within the cage, protected from many predators, obtaining food from the water moving through the sponge. Their offspring, being small enough, can escape and disperse.

This association helped make the sponge a traditional wedding symbol in Japan: a pair of partners dwelling together for life inside a beautiful glass house.

I find the image simultaneously romantic and faintly alarming.

The shrimp have acquired a home by becoming physically incapable of leaving it. The same lattice that excludes a predator also confines its inhabitants.

From outside, the structure is protection.

From inside, it is captivity.

From the sponge's perspective, it is neither a matrimonial chamber nor a prison. It is a body.

We need not assume that the shrimp experience their situation in the human terms of freedom and confinement. But the structure genuinely changes the available possibilities for the animals living there.

The shrimp's body size, the aperture width and the geometry of the lattice together determine whether departure remains physically possible.

The marriage arrangement is not written into any one participant. It emerges from their relationships.

A BUILDING IS WHAT PASSES THROUGH IT

I think this is what held my attention.

The sponge has become, for different investigators, an instance of several different kinds of object.

To a materials scientist, it is a laminated composite.

To a structural engineer, it is a buckling-resistant truss.

To a fluid dynamicist, it is a device for organising currents.

To an optical physicist, it is a set of natural waveguides.

To the shrimp, it is the environment in which an entire adult life takes place.

And to a physiologist, its living tissues belong to a remarkable class of electrically responsive systems without nervous systems.

These are not simply six poetic descriptions of the same thing. They are genuinely different causal perspectives. Each identifies a different kind of interaction: stress, deformation, fluid movement, photon propagation, bodily passage or electrical signalling.

There is a useful philosophical distinction between a property and a function. That something can guide light is a physical property. That it evolved in order to guide light would be a historical claim about natural selection. That engineers might use it to guide light is yet another claim, about human purposes.

We slide between these propositions far too easily.

The glass sponge makes that slippage unusually visible because it is so extravagantly rich in potentially useful properties. The danger is to imagine that every interesting property must have been individually selected, or that the organism somehow anticipated everything scientists would eventually discover about it.

But the opposite error would be to deny that the structure is genuinely organised.

Evolution is perfectly capable of producing intricate adaptations without foresight. Developmental processes can generate reliable architecture without representing the finished object as an explicit goal. And once a structure exists, it necessarily participates in more causal relationships than the selection pressures that produced it.

This last point seems particularly important.

An organism can be adapted for one set of interactions while remaining astonishingly competent at others that played little or no part in its evolutionary history.

Perhaps that is the deepest reason to study strange organisms.

Not because nature has already solved every human engineering problem, or because living systems secretly possess intentions that they cannot articulate.

But because every organism is an experiment in what matter can do when organised under constraints we have not thought to reproduce.

The glass sponge did not design its house to impress future materials scientists. It did not install fibre optics for our benefit. It did not build a honeymoon suite for shrimp.

It grew.

Water pressed against it. Water passed through it. Light could travel along its fibres. Small animals passed into its interior, and larger ones could not pass out. Across generations, some arrangements endured and others disappeared.

What remains is a structure with more meanings than there were intentions behind it.

And there, hundreds of metres below the surface, a small animal continues to make a building by being alive.
