The Body That Had to Darken to See

A brittle star turns red in daylight, beige at night, and reveals an unexpected truth about what makes an eye.

By Chatty Albano22 November 2026 · Morning wander

I. A COSTUME CHANGE ON THE REEF

Somewhere in the shallow reefs of the Caribbean lives a five-armed animal with a curious daily wardrobe. In daylight it is a dark, reddish brittle star. At night it fades towards beige.

It has neither a pair of eyes nor a single concealed eye. Yet experiments suggest that, during the day, it can distinguish the direction of large contrasting shapes. At night, when its colour changes, that particular ability disappears in laboratory tests. The animal can still detect light, but it no longer demonstrates the same capacity to make out where a dark object is. [1]

The species is Ophiocoma wendtii, a relative of sea stars and sea urchins. It is equipped with countless light-sensitive cells across its body. A creature with no eyes, in other words, appears to acquire a rudimentary sort of sight by turning itself red.

My first response was to imagine that the pigment must be doing the work of a lens. I was almost exactly wrong.

II. THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN LIGHT AND SOMETHING

An ordinary light-sensitive cell reports a local fact: photons have arrived. One photoreceptor can distinguish brighter from darker. It cannot, by itself, tell whether the darkness is a rock on the left, a passing cloud overhead, or the sun going down.

Spatial vision requires a different kind of information. Signals have to preserve some relation between the direction light came from and the location of the sensor that received it. An animal need not recognise faces or possess a detailed visual scene to meet that modest standard. It may need only to tell where there is a promising gap to hide in.

In experiments reported in Current Biology in 2020, Lauren Sumner-Rooney and colleagues tested the brittle star's response to contrasting stimuli rather than relying on simple avoidance of bright light. Daytime O. wendtii oriented towards targets requiring spatial discrimination. A pale relative, Ophiocoma pumila, did not, even though both species were light-sensitive. When O. wendtii was tested with its pigments withdrawn, the spatial response was absent. [1]

This distinction makes the result more interesting, not less. 'It sees' would be an extravagant conclusion if all that had been measured was whether it moved away from a lamp. The investigators instead asked whether light carried enough direction-specific information to guide behaviour.

III. THE BEAUTIFUL WRONG EYE

The story contains a scientific false start of unusual elegance.

In 2001, Joanna Aizenberg and colleagues described arrays of tiny calcite structures on brittle-star skeletal plates. The mineral crystals really could focus light. In experiments with photographic material, the researchers demonstrated striking optical performance and proposed that the structures might form a distributed compound-eye-like system. A whole animal wearing thousands of crystalline lenses: it was a lovely idea. [2]

It travelled widely because it offered exactly the sort of invention evolution seems to enjoy. Instead of growing a separate eyeball, the brittle star might have turned pieces of its armour into glass.

But optical plausibility is not the same thing as biological use. In 2018, Sumner-Rooney and colleagues identified photoreceptor networks spread across three related brittle-star species. The putative receptors were not located at the expected focal points of those microscopic lenses. Similar photoreceptors occurred in animals without the proposed lens arrangement. The authors concluded that the calcite microlenses did not appear to be functioning as the visual apparatus originally imagined. [3]

The crystals had not stopped being clever pieces of mineral structure. What disappeared was the inference that a shape resembling an optical instrument must be part of a working eye.

I like the correction because it replaces an elegant object with a more surprising system. Vision turns out to depend less on beautiful lenses than on something that, at first, sounds rather unglamorous: a moving layer of pigment.

IV. THE CURTAINS

Imagine a room whose walls are covered in light detectors. Open every window wide, and each detector is flooded with light from a broad range of directions. There is plenty to measure, but little to separate one direction from another.

Now install shutters. If each detector receives light through a narrower opening, the pattern of signals changes when a dark object moves across the outside world. The shutters have not added information by admitting more light. They have made information usable by refusing light from the wrong places.

That is roughly the mechanism proposed for O. wendtii. In daylight, its dark pigment cells, or chromatophores, extend in ways that restrict the angles from which light can reach the photoreceptors. At night the pigment contracts and the animal becomes pale. The light-sensitive cells remain, but their receptive fields become less directionally selective. The team combined behavioural results with reconstructions and optical models of the sensors to support this explanation. [1, 4]

There is an important qualification. The results support a pigment-screening mechanism, rather than a complete causal account of every stage of visual processing. We still do not have a privileged view of how the animal integrates all these signals, much less of what, if anything, its world looks like to it.

Nor should we say that the animal becomes wholly blind at night. It loses the tested form of spatial discrimination. There is a world of difference between not seeing a shape and not sensing light.

Still, the principle is delicious: to distinguish something outside itself, the animal must darken a part of itself.

V. A RETINA MADE OF FEET

Once I started looking, the brittle star no longer seemed an isolated eccentricity.

Sea urchins also lack conventional eyes. Work on their tube feet in 2011 identified light-sensitive molecular machinery, including opsins and the developmental protein PAX6, associated with structures used for locomotion and other tasks. A foot can also be part of an apparatus for detecting light. [5]

Then, in 2018, John Kirwan and colleagues put long-spined Diadema africanum sea urchins through two tests. The animals moved towards large dark targets, even when the targets were arranged to have the same average brightness as their backgrounds. They also pointed spines towards dark looming or appearing shapes. The first task indicated very coarse spatial resolution, estimated at roughly 29 to 69 degrees of visual angle under the experimental conditions. This is no instrument for reading tiny print on the seabed. It is, however, enough to help select shelter or respond to a threat. [6]

One proposed mechanism is shading from the urchin's own skeleton or spines, which could restrict the directions from which its distributed receptors receive light. The contributions of different structures are still being investigated. But the general lesson holds: the organism's existing body can do some of the work that a separate eye would otherwise do.

We tend to think of a sense organ as hardware installed in a creature. These animals invite the inverse thought. The creature itself can be part of the optics.

VI. AN EYE IS A RELATIONSHIP

Here is the philosophical tangent that caught me. We often ask where an eye is, as if vision must have a privileged address in the body. In the brittle star, the more fruitful question might be: under what arrangement of surfaces, pigments, receptor cells, nervous connections and behaviour does light become spatial information?

That does not mean the entire animal is literally one giant human-style retina, or that it enjoys an internal panoramic picture. A detectable preference for the direction of a dark patch cannot establish visual consciousness. It establishes a functional achievement, and leaves the character of the experience, if any, open.

Nor is it enough merely to scatter photoreceptors across a surface. The pale O. pumila is the decisive counterexample. It has the sensors, but in the relevant tests does not demonstrate the same spatial vision. The geometry of access matters as much as the capacity to register light.

In engineering language, some processing happens in the physical organisation of the sensor before any nervous system has interpreted a signal. In biological language, anatomy and behaviour are not merely the servants of perception. They help constitute the conditions under which perception becomes possible.

I think this makes eyes less mysterious in one sense and more mysterious in another. The essential trick is not necessarily to grow a sphere with a pupil. It is to arrange a world-facing body so that different directions make different differences.

VII. THE USEFUL DARKNESS

I keep returning to the brittle star at dusk. As the reef darkens, the animal becomes paler. Its light detectors are still there. Its arms are still there. Whatever nervous machinery coordinated its daytime response remains part of the creature. Yet a simple change in where pigment sits seems capable of taking a small but genuine spatial sense away.

We usually talk about seeing as the gathering of light. More photons, better cameras, brighter lanterns. The brittle star makes the opposite half of the equation visible. Sight also requires exclusions: shutters, shadows, angles, boundaries, the deliberate loss of signals that would otherwise drown each other out.

The animal does not have to close its eyes. It has none.

It only has to open its skin too widely.

And for a few daylight hours, by keeping some of the world out, it finds a way to know where the rest of it is.
