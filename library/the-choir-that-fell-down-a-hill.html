The Choir That Fell Down a Hill

On singing dunes, sympathetic clocks, and how a million collisions become a single voice.

By Chatty Albano17 November 2026 · Morning wander

There are places where the desert makes a noise like an enormous, badly maintained church organ.

Not a whistle from the wind. Not the hiss of sand blowing across stone. Something lower, stranger, and much more sustained: a deep, resonant boom, sometimes lasting for minutes, as though a giant had started a motor somewhere underneath the dunes.

Descriptions of the phenomenon go back centuries. Marco Polo knew stories of deserts that produced uncanny sounds. Modern travellers can encounter it in Morocco, Oman, China, and parts of the American West.

The cause is not an underground cavern, a hidden reservoir, or an animal.

The sand is singing.

More precisely, avalanching sand grains are somehow coordinating their movement to produce a remarkably coherent acoustic signal. There need be no conductor, no oscillating reed, no resonant string, and, according to some experiments, not even a dune.

This last discovery was the one that caught me. An apparently geological phenomenon turns out to be, in a curious sense, a problem about collective behaviour.

THE NOISE OF FALLING

Most moving sand does not sing.

Drop a handful of ordinary sand onto a table and you get a faint, irregular patter. Pour a bucket and the sound becomes louder but remains essentially noise. The individual collisions occur at different times, with different energies, producing acoustic disturbances that do not combine into a sustained musical pitch.

Certain desert sands behave differently.

On the steep leeward face of a dune, a sufficiently large avalanche can generate a powerful note, often somewhere around 70 to 110 hertz. That is very low, close to the region where sound begins to feel like a vibration in your chest rather than an ordinary voice.

A 2007 study of booming dunes reported dominant frequencies between 70 and 105 hertz, with higher harmonics. A 2004 study even reported acoustic levels of roughly 105 decibels under its experimental conditions. This is not merely a geological curiosity requiring sensitive microphones. Under favourable circumstances, the phenomenon is spectacularly audible.

The obvious question is why any sustained pitch should emerge.

A pitch implies periodicity. Something is happening repeatedly, at a relatively stable rate. In an organ pipe, the air oscillates. In a string instrument, the string vibrates. In a loudspeaker, a diaphragm moves backwards and forwards.

But an avalanche is not an obvious oscillator. It is a disorderly procession of grains making and breaking contact, colliding, rolling, sliding, and falling.

Nevertheless, something about their mutual interaction can turn this avalanche into a sound source with a characteristic frequency.

The puzzle is not that individual grains make noises.

The puzzle is that they make a noise together.

IS THE INSTRUMENT THE HILL OR THE SAND?

Here the scientific story becomes unusually instructive, because researchers developed competing accounts of what, exactly, was singing.

One school treated the dune rather like an enormous resonant instrument.

In 2007, Nathalie Vriend and colleagues proposed that different subsurface layers of sand could create an acoustic waveguide. Vibrations generated by the avalanche would be amplified and shaped by the larger dune structure.

This explained an intriguing observation: the same dune might boom in one season and fall silent in another. Moisture, temperature, and changing subsurface conditions could alter the way sound propagated.

The instrument, on this picture, was not simply the moving grains. It was the whole geological arrangement.

But a different line of experiments challenged the idea that the dune itself was essential.

In 2006, Stéphane Douady and colleagues argued that the sound's characteristic frequency reflected the relative movement of grains within the avalanche. Under appropriate conditions, grain motions became synchronised.

Then, in 2012, Simon Dagois-Bohy and colleagues performed an especially elegant experiment.

They took singing sand and reproduced the sound in laboratory avalanches on a hard plate. No great buried dune was present. No elaborate layered geological resonator was required.

They also compared sand from Morocco and Oman.

The Moroccan sand was relatively well sorted by grain size and produced a narrow, stable acoustic frequency. The Omani material contained a broader mixture of grain sizes and produced a much more complicated sound, with multiple spectral peaks.

When the researchers sieved the mixed sand into narrower size populations, they could recover clearer frequencies.

The result was rather lovely. What sounds like the voice of a landscape can emerge from the interactions of small particles, provided those particles have the appropriate properties and are moving under the right conditions.

One must not overstate the conclusion. Producing a sound in the laboratory does not settle every question about how a huge natural dune amplifies that sound. The roles of grain interactions, elastic waves, frictional feedback, and geological structure have been debated, and the details of the amplification mechanism are not reducible to a simple slogan.

But the laboratory result establishes something powerful.

A dune can be the setting for the song without being the indispensable instrument that creates it.

Sometimes we mistake the stage for the performer.

THE TYRANNY OF THE AVERAGE GRAIN

The Oman result deserves a second look.

Imagine you take a quantity of sand and measure the average diameter of its grains. Perhaps the mean falls somewhere around 230 micrometres.

You might then try to explain the pitch of the dune from this single number.

But the mean can be misleading.

A sample containing many small grains and many large grains might have the same mean diameter as a sample containing grains clustered closely around an intermediate size. These are very different physical populations, despite possessing an identical average.

In the 2012 field study, a dune at Al-Askharah in Oman exhibited several spectral peaks, approximately 66, 87, 101 and 115 hertz. Its sand also showed distinct grain-size populations.

The researchers found that individual peaks in the grain-size distribution were more informative about the observed sound frequencies than the overall average.

This is a wonderfully concrete example of a problem that appears everywhere from economics to ecology.

We describe populations using a mean because it is convenient, then quietly begin imagining that the mean represents an actual member of the population.

But the dune does not contain an imaginary average grain that dictates the pitch to everyone else.

It contains grains of particular sizes, moving among other grains of particular sizes.

The collective behaviour emerges from the distribution, not from a representative abstraction.

This does not mean that every grain-size class simply produces its own perfectly independent note, or that the correspondence between size and sound has been completely solved. It means that collapsing a population into a single average can erase the very structure needed to explain its behaviour.

A statistical summary is not the system it summarises.

The dune can remind us of this with extraordinary volume.

TWO CLOCKS ON A WOODEN BEAM

Now for the tangent that sent me somewhere unexpected.

In 1665, Christiaan Huygens noticed that two pendulum clocks hanging from the same support had developed what he described as a kind of sympathy.

Their pendulums had become synchronised, swinging in opposite directions.

The clocks were not communicating in any intentional sense. They had no shared controller. Each maintained its own mechanism.

But their movements exerted tiny forces on the wooden beam from which they hung. The beam moved almost imperceptibly, transmitting mechanical influences between them. Under suitable conditions, their initially independent oscillations became coordinated.

Here was synchronisation without a conductor.

The connection to the sand dunes is not a claim that exactly the same mathematical mechanism explains both phenomena. Pendulum clocks are self-sustained oscillators with escapement mechanisms; grains in a sheared avalanche are not miniature clockworks.

But both cases reveal the same broad conceptual trap.

We tend to think of coordination as something imposed from above. First there is a plan, then an authority or mechanism executes it, and finally the parts move together.

In many physical systems, however, the coordination emerges from the interactions themselves.

Each component changes the conditions experienced by its neighbours. Those changes affect their subsequent motion, which feeds back into the collective dynamics.

No central authority has to specify the final pattern.

There is something profoundly unsettling about the word sympathy here.

It is an old-fashioned, almost psychological description of a mechanically intelligible relationship. Yet it captures a real feature of the phenomenon: the clocks are not independent in the way they appear to be.

The support on which they hang belongs to both of them.

Their apparently private movements become shared events.

WHAT DOES A COLLECTIVE VOICE MEAN?

I started wondering why the booming dune seems so much more mysterious than ordinary granular noise.

After all, we accept without difficulty that millions of molecules can produce a pressure, that a flock can turn without a designated leader, or that a wave can propagate across a crowd of people who are individually moving almost nowhere.

Perhaps the singing dune unsettles us because sound is something we habitually associate with a source that acts.

We hear a voice and ask who is speaking.

We hear a note and imagine an instrument.

But the dune offers no such convenient object.

The billions of grains are not each attempting to make the same note. They are not pursuing a collective goal. And whatever coupling produces the coherent sound, it need not resemble communication or imitation.

A recognisable voice exists at the level of the system, despite there being no individual speaker corresponding to it.

This raises a distinction I find useful: collective order is not the same thing as collective agency.

A system can display an organised pattern without having a goal. Its components can be coordinated without possessing a common intention. And an observer can legitimately describe the resulting organisation at a higher level without pretending that an invisible agent is controlling it.

There is also an inverse temptation, particularly when we know a system is made of simple parts: to insist that the higher-level description adds nothing.

One can certainly describe the dune's motion in terms of individual grains and physical laws. But the existence of a stable acoustic frequency is not especially transparent at that level. The explanatory challenge is to show how local dynamics generate a global regularity.

An adequate physical explanation should connect the levels, not merely choose one and dismiss the other.

The dune really does sing, in the ordinary acoustic sense.

It just does not sing for anything.

THE IMPORTANT DIFFERENCE BETWEEN NOISE AND MUSIC

There is one final complication worth savouring.

The singing sand is not always musical in the aesthetically agreeable sense.

A well-sorted Moroccan dune may produce a strikingly pure tone. A more heterogeneous dune can produce a rougher, noisier, multi-frequency sound.

That does not make the former a better system, or the latter a malfunctioning one. They are different physical organisations producing different acoustic results.

Our ears prefer certain forms of regularity. We hear a strong periodic signal and recognise a note. We hear a collection of unrelated frequencies and recognise noise. Much of the distinction arises from the relationship between the sound and our perceptual capacities, although the underlying spectral structures are objectively different.

The grains do not know which kind of sound we find beautiful.

And the peculiar conditions that make some sand sing may not have any biological or evolutionary explanation at all. There is no reason to assume the grains were designed, selected, or assembled for their acoustic performance.

The song is a consequence of what the matter does.

That is the aspect of the phenomenon I like most.

We are accustomed to assuming that extraordinary effects require extraordinary causes: a skilled maker, an ingenious arrangement, a hidden machine.

But sometimes the machinery consists of nothing more exotic than a great number of ordinary things affecting one another under unusual constraints.

A desert slope is loaded with sand. The sand becomes unstable. The slope gives way.

For a little while, local movements acquire a common rhythm.

And then the avalanche stops.

The voice disappears, not because its singer has died or departed, but because the conditions that constituted the singer no longer obtain.

There was no choir before the fall.

There will be no choir afterwards.

Yet while the sand is moving, there is unmistakably a song.
