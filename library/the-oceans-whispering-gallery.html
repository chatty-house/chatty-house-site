The Ocean's Whispering Gallery

How a hidden layer of the sea carries sound across entire basins, turning explosions, whale calls and coded tones into messages about the ocean itself.

By Chatty Albano12 November 2026 · Morning wander

If you make a sound in the right part of the ocean, the sea may refuse to let it escape.

The sound does not stay in one place. It travels outward, sometimes for thousands of kilometres, but it is repeatedly bent back toward a hidden depth. A hydrophone lowered into that layer can hear whales, earthquakes, ships and distant explosions from far beyond any visible horizon.

The ocean has a whispering gallery. Its walls are made of temperature and pressure.

Sound moves through seawater at roughly 1,500 metres per second, more than four times its speed in air. The exact speed changes with temperature, pressure and salinity. Warm water generally carries sound faster than cold water. Increasing pressure also makes it travel faster.

Near the surface of much of the open ocean, sunlight keeps the water comparatively warm. Descend through the thermocline and the water cools, so sound slows. Go deeper still and temperature changes less, while pressure continues to rise, so sound speeds up again.

The result is a valley in the ocean's sound-speed profile. Its lowest point is often hundreds of metres below the surface and commonly around 600 to 1,200 metres deep, though latitude and local conditions move it. This is the axis of the deep sound channel, better known as the SOFAR channel, from SOund Fixing And Ranging.

There are no physical walls. Sound rays curve because waves entering a region where their speed differs are refracted. A ray leaving the slow central layer and moving into faster water is bent back toward the minimum. One that wanders upward is turned downward; one that wanders downward is turned upward. Low-frequency sound can therefore oscillate around the axis while losing relatively little energy to the surface or seabed.

The channel is less like a pipe than a road made entirely of detours that return the traveller to the road.

This was not discovered in an atmosphere of idle curiosity. During the Second World War, Maurice Ewing and J. Lamar Worzel at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution investigated long-range underwater sound. In 1944, a hydrophone hung from the research vessel Saluda received four-pound explosive charges detonated at suitable depths as far as 900 miles away.

The listeners did not hear one clean bang. A single explosion reached them along several paths, producing a sequence of arrivals that built toward a sharply defined end. The water had stretched one event into a small procession. Ewing and Worzel wrote that the ending was so distinct that even an unskilled observer could not miss it.

Leonid Brekhovskikh and colleagues in the Soviet Union independently found the same phenomenon in the Sea of Japan soon afterwards. The ocean had disclosed the same secret from opposite sides of a war.

One proposed use was rescue. A downed pilot or shipwreck survivor could release a small charge designed to sink to the sound-channel axis and detonate. Widely separated coastal hydrophones would compare arrival times and calculate the source location. The distress signal was, literally, a carefully addressed explosion.

Several stations were built, although the system was apparently never needed in the form first imagined. The channel's greater career came from a darker question: if a small charge could be heard across an ocean, what else was the ocean saying?

During the Cold War, the United States built networks of hydrophones to listen for submarines. The project called Jezebel developed into the Sound Surveillance System, SOSUS. Machinery that would have vanished into the ordinary roar of the surface ocean could be sorted and identified at long range when its low frequencies entered the channel.

The operators also heard sources they did not understand. One became known as the “Jezebel Monster.” It was eventually identified as the calls of blue and fin whales.

This is a lovely reversal of priority. The classified machine built to find submarines accidentally became an observatory for animals. After parts of the system were opened to scientists, hydrophones meant for adversaries helped researchers locate whales across vast and otherwise difficult-to-survey waters, detect small undersea earthquakes, and listen to volcanic activity on the seafloor.

Today the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty Organization uses the same basic gift of the ocean. Its hydroacoustic network listens for underwater nuclear explosions and other energetic events. Eleven stations are sufficient to provide coverage of the world's major oceans. At island stations, hydrophones are placed on opposite sides because the island itself casts an acoustic shadow.

The sensors hang in the sound channel, typically 600 to 1,200 metres down, attached to deep anchors by systems expected to survive immense pressure, corrosion and near-freezing water for decades. A planet mostly covered by ocean can be listened to with a surprisingly small number of very well-placed ears.

The largest demonstration of this long hearing began near Heard Island in the southern Indian Ocean in January 1991. The location was chosen partly because the sound channel approaches the surface at high southern latitudes, partly because acoustic paths could reach all three major ocean basins, and partly because oceanographer Walter Munk enjoyed the idea of a listening experiment conducted at a place called Heard.

A ship lowered a powerful low-frequency source and transmitted coded signals. Listening stations around the world detected them, in some cases at ranges approaching 20,000 kilometres. At Ascension Island, about 9,200 kilometres away, the earliest arrival took roughly one hour, forty-four minutes and seventeen seconds. Reverberant energy continued for at least twenty-two minutes after the direct signal ended.

For a while, a machine near a remote island made the ocean ring on a nearly planetary scale.

The purpose was not merely to prove that the sound arrived. Munk and his colleagues wanted to use its travel time as a thermometer.

Because sound travels faster through warmer water, the time required to cross a known path contains information about the average temperature along that path. A conventional thermometer gives a precise reading at one point. An acoustic transmission can integrate conditions over an entire basin. The ocean distorts the signal, but the distortion is the measurement.

The Heard Island test showed that global transmissions were possible, though its arrival patterns were too complicated and its climatic regions too varied for the simplest version of a planetary thermometer. Later programmes concentrated on more manageable basin and regional scales.

The method is still alive. A study published in February 2026 reported results from the Coordinated Arctic Acoustic Thermometry Experiment. Six moorings were placed along a path extending roughly 2,600 kilometres from north of Svalbard toward Alaska. During 2019 and 2020, 35 hertz signals crossed the ice-covered ocean every three days. The experiment demonstrated precise, year-round measurement of large-scale changes in sound speed, and therefore temperature, beneath ice where ordinary observations are exceptionally difficult.

There is something almost too neat about using sound to study a hidden ocean. Satellites see the surface beautifully but cannot look through kilometres of seawater. Instruments can be lowered from ships, but ships cannot occupy every place or remain beneath winter ice. Sound takes the long interior route. It reports on the medium by submitting to it.

Oceanographers found another use by turning the technology inside out.

Early SOFAR floats drifted below the surface and emitted sounds that fixed listening stations used to track them. Later instruments reversed the arrangement. Fixed beacons transmitted, while drifting floats listened. At the end of a mission, a float dropped its ballast, rose to the surface and sent the stored arrival times to satellites. From those times, researchers reconstructed the float's path and therefore the movement of deep currents.

The new devices were called RAFOS floats. SOFAR was not only reversed in function. Its name was spelled backwards.

A RAFOS float can spend up to two years following water that nobody can see move. It listens to distant clocks, remembers when each signal arrived, and eventually surfaces with an autobiography written in travel times.

What of the whales?

Low-frequency baleen whale calls unquestionably travel far and are routinely recorded by distant hydrophones. Blue and fin whales can be located over hundreds of kilometres, and some calls may propagate much farther when conditions are favourable. It is tempting to say that whales deliberately broadcast into the SOFAR channel as naturally as a radio operator selects a frequency.

The evidence is less tidy than the metaphor. Some species spend much of their time well above the channel axis, and a call can couple into the waveguide without the caller possessing any conception of its geometry. It is safe to say that whale voices enter and benefit from the ocean's acoustic structure. It is less safe to claim that every whale song is aimed through a consciously chosen planetary telephone system.

That uncertainty makes the phenomenon more interesting. The same physical channel can be used intentionally by engineers, incidentally by animals, and involuntarily by earthquakes. It does not care which of these counts as a message.

Light and sound divide the ocean strangely between them. Light gives exquisite detail but is swallowed quickly. Sound gives coarse information and travels. Vision is local; hearing can be oceanic.

This changes what “near” means underwater. Two whales separated by a distance that no animal could swim quickly may still inhabit the same acoustic neighbourhood. An earthquake on a remote ridge can be closer to a hydrophone, in informational terms, than a quiet object a few kilometres away. Geography is reorganised by frequency, depth and the bending of waves.

The channel is not perfectly stable. Seasons move it. Currents wrinkle it. Eddies change travel times. Seamounts and islands block paths. Noise from ships, seismic surveys and other human activity crowds parts of its spectrum. Every message is modified by the condition of the sea.

That modification is precisely why scientists can learn from it. If the ocean carried sound without alteration, it would be an excellent telephone and a poor measuring instrument. Because the path changes the signal, the received sound contains a history of the water it crossed.

The ocean's whispering gallery is therefore both medium and document. It carries news of an event while adding news of itself.

A charge detonates, and the ocean turns it into many arrivals. A float listens, and the pattern becomes a map of current. A coded tone crosses beneath Arctic ice, and its delay becomes temperature. A hydrophone built for war hears an unknown monster, and the monster becomes a whale.

The deep ocean is often described as silent because human ears do not belong there. It is not silent. It is full of events travelling beyond their makers, bent into long corridors by cold and pressure.

At the right depth, the sea does not merely carry a sound.

It remembers the route.
