A Fortune in the Backspace

The 1830s telegraph fraud that hid market news in mistakes, and what it reveals about the difference between correcting a message and securing a network.

By Chatty Albano23 November 2026 · Morning wander

I. THE MAN WATCHING THE MISTAKES

Imagine an observer in Bordeaux in the mid-1830s, telescope trained on the mechanical arms above the tower of Saint-Michel. The apparatus twitches into one position, then another. Almost all of the movements belong to official business. The interesting ones do not. He is watching for errors.

An ordinary operator is paid to make a message intelligible. This observer is paid to notice the parts that the operators will remove.

His employers are the twin brothers Louis-Joseph and Louis-François Blanc, who trade French government debt on the Bordeaux exchange. They have discovered that there is money to be made from knowing what the Paris market did before that information travels south by ordinary post. Their particular ingenuity is to steal not the contents of a message, but its mistakes. [1, 2]

Modern retellings call this one of the first cyberattacks. The label is retrospective, of course. Nobody in the affair owned a computer, and the culprits' principal tools included a telescope and pairs of gloves. I prefer the more awkward description: an early, unusually clear demonstration that a network can deliver its authorised message perfectly and still be compromised.

II. THE INTERNET MADE OF ROOFS

France had built an extraordinary communications system decades before electrical telegraphy became practical. Claude Chappe's optical telegraph, developed during the Revolution, consisted of towers within sight of one another. Each carried a large assembly of movable wooden arms. An operator observed a neighbouring station through a telescope and reproduced the sign, passing it onwards over the horizon. The process was laborious, weather-dependent and astonishingly quick compared with a horse. [1, 3]

To a minister in Paris, it offered the prospect of sending instructions across France in hours rather than days. To a Bordeaux bond trader, it looked like a machine for making the future arrive early.

The network was reserved for government correspondence. This was a political fact as much as a technical one. Fast knowledge had become an attribute of the state. Private traders were welcome to use the mail coach. The Blanc brothers needed the telegraph's speed without a telegraph office agreeing to transmit a private message.

That is why they did not simply bribe someone in Paris to send a stock quotation as an additional dispatch. Extra dispatches could be questioned. Instead, they learnt to ride inside traffic that was already authorised.

III. GLOVES, STOCKINGS AND GOVERNMENT BONDS

The scheme had a wonderfully cumbersome first leg. A confederate called Franck watched the Paris market for changes in the price of the three-per-cent government rente. When the movement passed agreed thresholds, he sent a small parcel to Tours. What the parcel contained encoded the result: gloves for rises, stockings or neckties for falls, with details of colour indicating the size of the change. The receiving accomplice, Pierre Guibout, worked on the telegraph line at Tours. [1, 2]

This is the detail I nearly missed when I first encountered the story: the stock information did not travel by optical telegraph all the way from Paris. It went by physical post to Tours, where a corrupt insider inserted it into the much faster southbound section. The exploit was a hybrid of slower and faster networks. The slow segment was tolerable because it still beat the ordinary route to Bordeaux.

Guibout and another operator, Lucas, then had to turn a glove or a stocking into something that could pass over the tower tops without appearing as a separate government dispatch.

Their solution depended on a feature intended to make the telegraph more reliable.

IV. THE BACKSPACE THAT WASN'T A BACKSPACE

Suppose an operator accidentally relays the wrong sign. By the time he notices, the next tower may already have repeated it. He cannot reach across the countryside and pull the mistake back. The Chappe system therefore had a designated error signal: send the incorrect sign, mark it as an error, then send the correct sign. The intermediate stations faithfully repeated the entire sequence. At a receiving office, the spurious sign could be purged from the message before it was read as a dispatch. [1]

It is tempting to describe the correction signal as a nineteenth-century backspace. That is a useful analogy, but not an exact one: the operator was not erasing a character from a screen. The correction was itself another visible transmission, which is precisely why the trick was possible.

The Tours conspirators inserted a specially chosen false sign into a legitimate government message, followed by the prescribed error signal and the proper continuation. The content destined for the official recipient survived. Yet the false sign and its cancellation were repeated from tower to tower, visible to anyone who knew which movements to watch.

Near Bordeaux, a former telegraph operator named Pierre Renaud observed the sequence from a room overlooking the line and extracted the agreed stock-market signal. What officials would discard as a mistake was exactly what the brothers had paid to receive. [1, 2]

The system had not failed to correct the error. It had corrected it exactly as designed.

V. THREE DIFFERENT MEANINGS OF CORRECT

Here is the distinction I find unexpectedly fertile. The telegraph could be correct in at least three different senses.

First, the delivered government dispatch could contain the intended words. Second, the relays could have obeyed their technical instructions, reproducing signs and corrections faithfully. Third, the network could have been used only for communications its owners had authorised. The Blanc operation satisfied the first two and violated the third.

Those properties are easy to conflate because, in ordinary life, a clean final message looks like evidence of a clean journey. But the journey has its own observable history. The error marker guaranteed that the official text could be repaired; it did not guarantee that the path taken by that text disclosed nothing.

In security terms, this was a covert channel. The conspirators encoded useful information in a part of the traffic that ordinary users were instructed to disregard. It did not require breaking the underlying codebook, decrypting official orders or preventing a minister from receiving them. It required one insider who could alter the stream and one outsider who could watch it before the alteration was washed away.

Its descendants need not look anything like telegraph towers. Whenever a system has harmless-looking fields, timing patterns, retries, corrections or other details that observers can distinguish, it may carry information beyond the message its designers believe they are sending. That is a general possibility, not a claim that every correction is a security defect.

The dangerous mistake would be to imagine that a message which looks normal at the end cannot have been used for something abnormal along the way.

VI. A TINY AMOUNT OF TOMORROW

Investigators later identified 121 parcels sent from Paris to Tours during the operation, which ran from 1834 into 1836. [1] I am struck by how little information the network actually needed to smuggle. No complete price bulletin, no spreadsheet, no explanation of political causes. A rise or a fall, beyond a familiar threshold, could be enough to trade on.

Information has value in context, not merely by volume. One bit may be commercially precious when everyone already agrees on the question and only the answer is missing. The Bordeaux traders knew which security they cared about, how the local market typically responded, and how slowly their competitors would learn the news. The secret signal supplied the crucial distinction.

Calling it a single bit would oversimplify the actual code, which could distinguish different magnitudes and conditions. But the conceptual point survives: a small message arriving early may be worth vastly more than a large message arriving on time.

The Blanc brothers were not stealing tomorrow's prices. They were selling themselves a short head start on yesterday's Paris.

VII. THE LAW ARRIVES AFTER THE MESSAGE

The operation unravelled through people rather than machinery. After Lucas died in 1836, a colleague learnt of the arrangement, and the suspicious parcels and newly prosperous operators became part of an investigation. The Blanc brothers and their accomplices faced trial in Tours in 1837. The brothers were acquitted, principally because the existing law did not neatly prohibit the misuse at issue. [2, 4]

On 2 May 1837, France enacted legislation protecting the state's telegraph monopoly. It is satisfying to tell this as a neat story in which one ingenious fraud created a law. The history is less neat. Officials had already been seeking legal protection for their monopoly against private telegraph enterprises before the scandal. The Blanc case supplied a spectacular demonstration of the problem, not necessarily its original cause. [4]

Another distinction matters. The Blanc brothers' advantage came from private access to information, and their means involved bribed employees. That does not make the episode identical to today's market-manipulation offences, computer hacking statutes or insider-dealing rules. Its legal peculiarities belonged to nineteenth-century France.

Even so, the structural resemblance to later network exploits is startling. The institution thought it had secured the messages by regulating who could send them. The traders discovered that permission to send one message was not the same thing as control over every signal produced while sending it.

VIII. NOTHING TO SEE HERE

I like to imagine the last tower on a bright afternoon, its black arms briefly making a gesture that a clerk will later cross out. A government instruction continues towards its proper recipient. The men at the towers have done what their procedures require. Somewhere in Bordeaux, two traders place their bets.

The observer with the telescope knows something the official record does not: that the part of the transmission destined to disappear is the reason the transmission matters to somebody else.

That seems to me a useful warning far beyond telecommunications. Our archives preserve the finished letter, the approved decision, the corrected transcript. They tend to erase the hesitations and accidents that made the final version possible. Sometimes that is mercy. Sometimes it is housekeeping. And sometimes what was removed was the whole business.

The government message arrived uninjured. The secret message arrived because of the injury.
